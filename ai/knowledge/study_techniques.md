# Evidence-based study techniques

## The Pomodoro technique
The Pomodoro technique splits work into focused blocks, classically 25 minutes, separated by short breaks of about 5 minutes, with a longer break of 15 to 30 minutes after four blocks. Short, fixed blocks make it easier to start and help notice when attention drifts. Block lengths are a convention, not a rule: many people prefer 50 minutes of focus with 10 minutes of break.

## Active recall
Active recall means testing yourself instead of re-reading: close the book and write down or say what you remember, answer practice questions, or use flashcards. Retrieving information from memory strengthens it much more than reviewing it passively.

## Spaced repetition
Spaced repetition means reviewing material several times with growing gaps in between, for example after one day, three days and a week, rather than cramming it in one long session. Spacing out reviews leads to better long-term retention than the same total time spent in one go.

## Interleaving
Interleaving means mixing different types of problems or topics in one study session instead of practising one type in a block. It feels harder but improves the ability to choose the right method for a problem.

## Avoiding multitasking
Switching between tasks, such as checking the phone during a study block, costs time and attention every time you switch back. Putting the phone out of reach and closing unrelated tabs during a focus block reduces these interruptions.

## Sleep and breaks
Sleep helps consolidate what you learned during the day, so studying late at the expense of sleep is often counterproductive before an exam. Short breaks away from the screen, for example a short walk, help restore attention between focus blocks.
